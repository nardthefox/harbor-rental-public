KPIs of a successful rental

These are the measures most investors check first. None of them is a guarantee. Treat them as a common language for “does this property pay its way?”

  • Monthly cash flow is what’s left after operating costs and the mortgage. A rental that pays you is easier to hold than one you have to feed.
  • Cash-on-cash return is that annual cash flow divided by the cash you actually put in (down payment, closing costs, fees, and any upfront rehab / CapEx). Many investors use something in the 8–12% range as a starting screen for a leveraged rental — your target may be different.
  • Cap rate is the property’s yield if you paid cash: yearly net operating income ÷ purchase price. It lets you compare two properties without mixing in a loan. Comfortable ranges depend on the market and the risk.
  • DSCR (debt service coverage) asks whether the rent covers the housing payment. Many DSCR lenders start around 1.00× (rent equals the payment); some want 1.20–1.25× for better terms. A cash purchase has no DSCR.
  • The 1% rule is a quick first filter: monthly rent around 1% of the price. It ignores taxes, repairs and the loan, and it’s often hard to hit in high-price markets. Use it to sort, not to decide.
  • Expense ratio is the share of rent that goes to vacancy and operating costs — not the mortgage. A common planning shortcut, often called the “50% rule,” is that about half of the rent may be eaten by those costs over time. Another published shortcut (the “55% rule”) plans for about 45% of rent to go to expenses, leaving 55% as net operating income.
  • Rules of thumb vary. A tight cash-flow market may reward appreciation more than yield. A lender’s minimum is not the same as your goal. Always check local rents, taxes and actual loan terms.

Terms used on this page

Net operating income (NOI)
Rent plus any other income, minus vacancy and operating costs: property tax, insurance, the maintenance + CapEx reserve, management and other monthly costs. It leaves out the mortgage payment.
PITI and PITIA
Principal, interest, property tax and insurance, the full monthly housing payment. PITIA adds association (HOA) dues; this page also counts any monthly mortgage insurance in it. The DSCR card uses rent ÷ PITIA.
Cash invested
Down payment plus closing costs, points and loan fees, and any upfront rehab / CapEx. It is the bottom half of the cash-on-cash return.
Amortization
The number of years the monthly payment is set to pay the loan off over. A longer amortization means a lower payment and slower paydown.
Balloon payment
The loan balance still owed when a balloon comes due, paid in one lump sum, often by refinancing or selling. The monthly payment is still figured over the full amortization.
Example APR
The cost of credit as a yearly rate. It counts interest plus prepaid finance charges such as points, loan fees and upfront mortgage insurance, figured in the spirit of Regulation Z, Appendix J. Here it is an estimate for education that excludes taxes and insurance. It is not a rate quote or offer of credit.
Vacancy
The share of income you expect to lose to empty months and turnover between tenants.
Maintenance + CapEx reserve
A monthly set-aside, as a percent of rent, for repairs and for big replacements such as a roof, furnace or water heater. It is separate from one-time upfront rehab.
Principal paydown
The part of each loan payment that lowers the balance. It builds equity even if the property’s value does not change.
Equity
Estimated property value minus what is still owed on the loan. The five-year snapshot under More detail shows an estimate, not a projection to bank on.
FHA house hack
Buying a 2–4 unit property with an FHA loan and living in one unit. Here that unit’s rent is not counted as income. FHA loans carry mortgage insurance premiums.
DSCR loan
An investor loan that qualifies mainly on whether the property’s rent covers its payment, rather than on your personal income. Terms vary by lender.
